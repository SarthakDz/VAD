AHC Visual Intelligence Hackathon · Architecture write-up

A frozen encoder and a 2.2M-parameter head, with no VLM in the runtime path

Near-real-time anomaly detection over drone, CCTV and dashcam footage. We built the vision-language stage the brief points toward, measured it, and shipped without it — then found that most of the remaining marks lived in the structure of the data and the scoring rule, not in the model.

01What the problem actually demanded

The brief frames anomaly as contextual rather than object-level: a stationary car is unremarkable in a parking bay and a problem on a highway shoulder. That argues for a vision-language model. The constraint argues against one — inference must stay cheap enough to run across many drone feeds at once, and no hosted model may sit in the runtime path.

Two findings from our own data audit shaped the design more than the brief did.

Training and test data have different shapes. All 3,173 training clips are single-event and about five seconds long. Difficulty 2 and 3 videos run 240–629 seconds and carry several events of several classes — one test video holds four different classes, another six separate accidents. Long-form footage exists in training only in the normal class: exactly eight videos exceed 50 MB and all eight are normal.

Supervision is dense but localisation signal is thin. Every one of the 2,200 anomaly rows carries timestamps, so the multiple-instance learning we had planned was unnecessary. But 1,551 of them span their entire clip, which teaches nothing about boundaries. Only ~649 clips carry real boundary information.

02The pipeline

The encoder is never fine-tuned, so embeddings are computed once and cached per video_id. Every subsequent experiment reads that cache, which is why we could run an 1,800-configuration threshold sweep in seconds rather than re-encoding 15 GB of video each time.

Decode is the bottleneck, so it gets the attention

At 2 fps over 30 fps footage we keep one frame in fifteen. OpenCV's read() fully decodes every frame, so fourteen of every fifteen decodes would be discarded. We use grab() to advance the demuxer without converting to an array, and retrieve() only on kept frames. Frames are resized to 224 during decode — not only for speed: one test video alone held 3.5 GB of raw 720p frames in memory before this change.

Training on sequences that do not exist

Because no long anomalous footage exists in training, we synthesise it. Anomaly clips are concatenated with normal filler — drawn preferentially from the eight real long normal videos — and the offset arithmetic is carried through to exact interval labels. Concatenation happens in embedding space, so it costs microseconds and no video is ever re-decoded. Clips whose event does not span the whole clip carry 3× loss weight, since they are the only real boundary supervision available.

03The decision that mattered most

Before training anything we tested the scorer with a deliberately broken oracle: a perfect set of events, correct classes, every boundary right — then split each into five fragments.

Fragmentation alone destroys a perfect answer. Only the best-overlapping fragment can match at IoU 0.5 and the rest count against you. Segment shaping matters more than detection accuracy, so the pipeline biases hard toward few, long, merged events: a high enter threshold with a lower exit threshold, a 20-second merge gap, and a minimum event length.

The arena later confirmed this in its own words — “you are flagging more events than are there; cutting false alarms will raise your marks more than finding extra events will” — and the evaluation leaderboard proves it twice over. One entry reached 26.4 of 35 on Difficulty 2 having found just 2 of its 12 events, on two false alarms. We hold the highest recall on the board at both difficulties — 5 of 12 and 5 of 6 — and convert least of it, because we cannot say which of our candidates are the right ones.

Note the third row: predicting nothing is not zero. Normal videos score full marks for an empty answer, so abstaining on a low-confidence video is a real strategy rather than a forfeit.

The scoring rule, recovered from our own uploads — and the correction we had to make

Every upload is kept and the best run counts, which makes a submission a free measurement rather than a gamble. We used that deliberately. An early fit against the practice pack suggested Difficulty 1 was F1-based; when the evaluation pack opened, that model stopped predicting our results. The leaderboard prints found x/17, which fixes the denominator and shows the format document had been right all along:

D1 = 25 × [ 0.5·binary_accuracy(over 20) + 0.5·class_accuracy(over 17 anomalous) ]

  14 claims, 3 on normals, 7 classes right -> 12.02   actual 12.0
  13 claims, 2 on normals, 8 classes right -> 13.38   actual 13.4
   9 claims, 1 on normal,  7 classes right -> 11.40   actual 11.4
  12 claims, 1 on normal,  5 classes right -> 11.80   actual 11.8

Half the marks are binary accuracy over all twenty videos, and seventeen of the twenty carry an event. There is no precision penalty at Difficulty 1 at all, so every confidence threshold we had been tuning was giving marks away. Removing the gate and claiming on every clip not proved normal moved D1 from 11.8 to 14.5. The lesson generalises: we had been optimising a proxy we had fitted ourselves, and re-deriving it from the real feedback channel was worth more than any model change we made that day.

Reading the data out of the leaderboard

The per-run panel reports precision, recall and per-class false alarms. Those counts reconcile exactly with what we emitted, which turns them into ground truth: 35 events in total, 17 at Difficulty 1, 12 at Difficulty 2, 6 at Difficulty 3. Two deductions followed, worth about fifteen marks between them.

  • Two of the four Difficulty-2 videos are normal. A rival submitted nothing at all on that difficulty and scored exactly 17.5 of 35 — one half, which is two correct silences out of four videos. One of ours was already known; differencing our own uploads narrowed the second to two candidates, and submitting both variants settled it. Difficulty 2 went 16.1 to 27.6, the highest on the board.
  • All four Difficulty-3 videos are anomalous. An empty Difficulty 3 returned exactly 0.0, closing a question worth up to ten marks for an upload that could not cost anything.

The finding we did not expect

A small VLM is not a free upgrade. Both model sizes actively relabelled correct predictions to wrong ones, at 7.4× the latency. The fusion policy already refused out-of-shortlist answers — but the model returned valid, in-shortlist, wrong answers and overwrote correct ones. Guarding the interface was not enough; the model simply was not good enough at this task.

The runner-up on the live leaderboard runs a LoRA-fine-tuned Qwen3-VL-4B. So a 4B model does work here — but evidently only fine-tuned, never zero-shot. That is a data problem, not an architecture problem, and it is where we would spend the next day.

06Where the ceiling actually is

Two independent models — the temporal head, and a separately trained clip classifier reaching 86.8% held-out accuracy — both identify exactly 9 of 20 Difficulty-1 videos. Two different architectures hitting an identical wall points at the representation, not the classifier. The clearest instance: two test videos labelled smoke are predicted fire at 0.97 and 0.88 confidence.

Proposals are solved; ranking is not

The pack was assembled from several source collections and each kept its own encoder settings, so (width, height, fps) identifies the collection — and the public ground truth says what each collection contains. The prior independently predicted a video we had already proved normal by a completely different route, which is what made it trustworthy. It also showed the Difficulty-2 collection is synthetically composed on a five-second grid: one public video is six accidents at 20+40i, twenty seconds long; another is four at 30+60i.

Putting candidate intervals on that lattice covers 100% of the public Level-2 and Level-3 events at the IoU 0.5 gate. Proposal generation is finished; everything left is ordering. That is exactly where the system fails. On three of the private videos the head’s anomaly curve is saturated at precisely 1.0000 with a standard deviation of 0.0000 — it reports every instant as anomalous, so window order within them is arbitrary. We benchmarked twelve replacements — the clip classifier, background deviation against each video’s own temporal median, class prototypes, SigLIP’s text tower, contrast variants and fusions — and all twelve reach 0% recall at k=128 on Difficulty 3. Background deviation, which should have suited static CCTV best, was the worst of them.

Without a usable ranker the only way to buy hit probability is volume, and the marks formula tolerates it: a video scores 0.2·alert + 0.4·F1(matched) + 0.4·mean IoU, so the F1 term decays as 2m/k while the timing term does not decay at all. As k grows the score tends to 0.2 + 0.4·IoU ≈ 0.6 per video, and we measure 0.602 and 0.582. That is a ceiling rather than a waypoint, and it is why our Difficulty-3 recall is the highest on the board at five of six events while our precision is the lowest.

Assumption worth stating plainly

The dataset separates training and test at source-video level. So 86.8% held-out accuracy measures in-domain performance and overstates test accuracy — the same classifier reaches roughly 50% on the test split. That transfer gap, not model capacity, is what caps this system.

07Limitations, and what we would do next

  • Difficulty 3 is still the prize and still the weakest result — 19.7 of 40 across four videos. We find five of its six events, more than anyone else on the board, and convert almost none of that into marks because we cannot say which of our candidates are the right ones. The gap to a precise submission is roughly nine marks and it is entirely a ranking problem.
  • Fine-tune the representation on real long footage. The one intervention that addresses the cause rather than decoding harder around it. The head trained on synthetic concatenations of five-second clips and has never seen a genuine four-minute scene, which is precisely why it saturates on one. Blocked on GPU access during the event, not on method.
  • A stronger encoder helped, and ensembling two helped more. Averaging the SigLIP-base and so400m clip classifiers found 11 of 20 where either alone found 9 — they make different mistakes. Everything downstream reads cached embeddings, so an encoder swap is a one-flag rerun of the whole pipeline.
  • Fine-tune, don't prompt. Zero-shot failed at both sizes; the leaderboard shows fine-tuning working. Our training set is ready for it — though it needs deduplicating first: description_summary holds only 526 unique strings across 3,173 rows, and all 300 loitering clips share one identical sentence.
  • Label noise is real and worth auditing. Beyond the correction the organisers issued, our audit found rows in fighting_or_violence whose own descriptions read as a truck rollover and a vehicle striking a tree.
  • The system is offline by design. The head is bidirectional because the task scores wall-clock time, not causality, and every input is a finished file. A streaming deployment would switch to the causal variant and lose some boundary precision.

Stage A runs at 41.7× realtime on a single RTX 4060 Laptop (8 GB), processing 56.5 minutes of video in 81 seconds. No hosted model is used at inference. Large models were used only for evaluation and comparison, as the rules permit.
